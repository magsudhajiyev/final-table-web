## The storyline: a 22-year-old with a monster lead

The headline is **Lucas Jumalon** — 22 years old, American, and sitting on a
chip lead so large it's almost unfair. With ~194M he holds more than double
the runner-up and roughly a third of all the chips in play. A stack that
dominant at a Main Event final table is rare, and it hands him an enormous
edge: he can apply pressure relentlessly, force the short stacks into brutal
decisions, and rarely be at risk himself.

But poker doesn't hand out trophies for chip counts. A big lead is a *weapon*,
not a guarantee — final tables have flipped on a single cooler more times than
anyone can count. The chasers, including veterans like **Greg Mueller**, know
that one double-up changes the whole complexion of the table.

## Why the final table is a different game

If you only ever watch one form of poker, watch a Main Event final table —
because the strategy shifts completely from the earlier days:

- **ICM pressure is everything.** With pay jumps of hundreds of thousands (and
  eventually millions) of dollars between finishing positions, chips stop
  being worth their face value. A short stack can be "correct" to fold a
  strong hand that they'd snap-call in a cash game, purely because busting now
  costs a fortune in equity.
- **The big stack bullies.** Jumalon can open-raise a huge share of hands and
  put the medium stacks in miserable spots, because *they* have more to lose
  by tangling with him than he does.
- **Short stacks play push-or-fold.** Below ~20 big blinds, the game
  collapses into a shove-or-fold Nash game — no more postflop finesse, just
  spot selection and timing.

That's the beauty of it: nine players, one table, and a strategic problem that
even the world's best get wrong under the lights.
